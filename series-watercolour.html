Painting Series

Watercolour Studies

Intimate scenes rendered in watercolour—portable, spontaneous, and technically precise. These works reveal a different side of Sem's practice: quiet observation rather than monumental documentation.

The Watercolour Studies represent a more intimate mode of working for Loh Ek Sem. Unlike the large-scale acrylic paintings of the Malay Kampung and Peranakan series, these watercolours are small enough to carry outdoors, painted on location or from quick reference sketches.

Watercolour demands a different discipline than acrylic. The medium is unforgiving—mistakes cannot be painted over, layers must be planned in advance, and the artist must work quickly before the paint dries. But these constraints also produce a kind of spontaneity that acrylic cannot match.

The series includes 8 works, ranging from 38 × 50cm to 76 × 57cm. Subjects include everyday scenes: a chess game in progress (Chesstime), laundry drying by the river, quiet domestic moments. These are not grand cultural statements but observations of daily life—the kind of scenes that might be overlooked but reveal texture and rhythm when rendered with care.

Chesstime

38 × 50cm, Watercolour on paper, 1998

Two players absorbed in a chess match—the concentration visible in their postures, the board positioned on a makeshift table. A scene that could be from any Malaysian neighborhood, past or present.

Technical Notes

Why Watercolour Matters

Watercolour is often considered a "sketching" medium—preliminary work before the "real" painting. But Sem treats watercolour as a complete practice in its own right. The transparency of the medium, the way colors bleed into one another, the necessity of planning layers in advance—these are not limitations but expressive tools.

Technique

  • Wet-on-wet — Applying wet paint to wet paper, allowing colors to flow and blend naturally. Used for skies, water, and soft backgrounds.
  • Wet-on-dry — Applying wet paint to dry paper for sharp edges and fine detail. Used for figures, architectural elements, and foreground objects.
  • Lifting — Removing paint with a clean brush or tissue to create highlights. Watercolour cannot be painted over with white, so the white of the paper must be preserved or lifted.
  • Glazing — Multiple thin layers of transparent color, each allowed to dry before the next. Builds depth and richness without losing luminosity.

Watercolour vs. Acrylic

Acrylic (used in the Malay Kampung and Peranakan series) is opaque, forgiving, and slow-drying. It allows for reworking, layering, and correction. Watercolour is transparent, unforgiving, and fast-drying. It demands confidence and planning. Both mediums appear in Sem's practice, each chosen for what it can achieve that the other cannot.
