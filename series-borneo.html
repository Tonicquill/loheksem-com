Painting Series

Borneo Series

The vibrant culture of Sarawak's Dayak communities — hornbill dances, sape music, traditional weaving, and the longhouse way of life — documented through 40+ paintings.

The Borneo Series (1998–2010) represents Loh Ek Sem's most extensive ethnographic undertaking. Over twelve years, Sem traveled repeatedly to Sarawak, documenting the vanishing traditions of the Iban, Bidayuh, and Orang Ulu communities through oil and acrylic painting.

The series captures ceremonies that predate written history: the Hornbill Dance (a warrior's welcome), the Gawai harvest festivals, the intimate moments of longhouse life where elders teach children the patterns of traditional pua kumbu weaving. These are not romanticized depictions but documentary records of a culture in transition.

The series includes 40+ major works, ranging from intimate portraits (73 × 98cm) to monumental canvases (154 × 116cm). All paintings are executed in oil and acrylic, chosen for their ability to capture both the vibrant colors of ceremonial dress and the subtle textures of weathered wood and woven bamboo.

Cultural Context

The Longhouse Community

The longhouse (rumah panjai) is the traditional dwelling of Sarawak's Dayak peoples. A single structure can house 50-100 families, each with a private apartment (bilik) opening onto a communal veranda (ruai).

  • Ruai — The communal veranda running the length of the longhouse. This is where festivals are held, guests are received, and community decisions are made.
  • Bilik — Individual family apartments. Each bilik is autonomous but bound to the community through shared rituals and mutual obligation.
  • Tanju — The open-air platform at the front of the longhouse. Used for drying rice, peppers, and laundry; also the stage for certain dances.

Dayak Performing Arts

Dance and music are inseparable from Dayak spirituality. Every major life event — birth, marriage, death, harvest — is marked by performance that connects the community to the spirit world.

  • Hornbill Dance (Tari Engkerambong) — A warrior's welcome dance. The rhinoceros hornbill is sacred to the Dayak, representing the upper world. Feathers are worn only by those who have earned the right through bravery.
  • Sape — The boat lute of the Orang Ulu. Traditionally played for healing ceremonies, the sape has been adopted by contemporary musicians while retaining its spiritual significance.
  • Gendang — Drums that communicate with spirits. Different rhythms call different entities; the gendang rayah is used specifically for summoning protective ancestors.
  • Pua Kumbu — Ceremonial woven cloth. The patterns are not decorative but narrative — each design tells a story known only to the weaver and those she teaches.

Gawai — The Harvest Festival

Gawai Dayak is celebrated at the end of the rice harvest (May-June). The festival gives thanks to Sempulang Gana, the god of agriculture, and welcomes the spirits of ancestors who return to visit the living.

Key rituals include:

  • Miring — Offering ceremony. Small portions of food and tuak (rice wine) are placed at the four corners of the longhouse to appease malevolent spirits.
  • Ngajat — The dance of welcome. Performed by young men and women in full ceremonial dress, the ngajat is both entertainment and spiritual offering.
  • Banyo — Ritual bathing. The community processes to the river to cleanse themselves of misfortune before the new agricultural cycle begins.

Why This Series Matters

Modernization has transformed Sarawak since the 1970s. Longhouses have been replaced with individual homes, traditional dress is worn only for festivals, and young people migrate to cities for work. The knowledge held by elders — weaving patterns, dance steps, oral histories — risks disappearing within a generation.

Sem's paintings document a culture in transition. They are neither nostalgic nor judgmental but serve as visual archives for future generations who may never experience the longhouse way of life firsthand. The works ask: What is lost when tradition yields to modernity? What is gained? These questions are embedded in the brushstrokes, even when not explicitly stated.
