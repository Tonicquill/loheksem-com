Painting Series

Bali Series

The grace, spirituality, and theatrical beauty of Balinese dance — from the sacred Legong to the epic Ramayana Ballet — captured through oil and acrylic painting.

The Bali Series (1996–2005) represents Loh Ek Sem's deep engagement with Indonesian performing arts. Drawn to the island's rich ceremonial culture and the codified gestures of traditional dance, Sem produced a body of work that functions as both aesthetic appreciation and ethnographic documentation.

Each painting captures dancers in moments of suspended grace — the flickering eye movements (seledet), the angular wrist rotations, the intricate footwork that connects performer to earth and spirit. These are not tourist snapshots but studied observations of an art form that requires decades of training.

The series includes 7 major works, ranging from intimate solo portraits (73 × 98cm) to ensemble scenes (130 × 80cm). Paintings are executed in both oil and acrylic, chosen for their ability to capture the luminous quality of Balinese costumes and the dramatic intensity of performance.

Cultural Context

Balinese Dance Traditions

Balinese dance is inseparable from Hindu-Balinese spirituality. Every gesture, eye movement, and foot placement is codified — a visual language developed over centuries of religious and artistic refinement.

  • Legong — Classical dance performed by young women before puberty. Characterized by intricate finger movements, rapid head turns, and the signature seledet (side-to-side eye dart). Dancers train from age 5-7.
  • Ramayana Ballet — Dance-drama depicting the Hindu epic. Performances can last entire nights, with dancers entering trance states to embody divine characters.
  • Reong — Synchronized dance performed by groups of four or more dancers. The challenge is perfect unison — no individual expression, only collective grace.
  • Kipas (fan) — Ceremonial prop used in many dances. The fan extends the dancer's gestures, creating larger visual narratives through stylized movement.

The Dancer's Body

Balinese dance demands extreme physical discipline. Key characteristics include:

  • Agem — The basic stance: knees bent, weight low, torso upright. Dancers maintain this position throughout performance, requiring immense leg strength.
  • Seledet — Rapid side-to-side eye movement. This is not natural but trained — dancers practice for years to achieve the sharp, darting quality.
  • Udjet — Head movement that counterbalances the torso. The head tilts opposite to the body's lean, creating dynamic tension.
  • Ngrayud — Finger extension. Every finger is pulled back to its limit, creating the characteristic lotus-bud hand shape.

Why This Series Matters

Tourism has transformed Bali since the 1970s. Traditional dances, once performed only in temples for religious ceremonies, are now staged nightly for tourists. Some argue this commodification dilutes the spiritual essence; others see it as economic survival for an art form that might otherwise disappear.

Sem's paintings document dancers in their full ceremonial regalia — not as tourist attractions but as practitioners of a sacred art. The works ask: What is lost when ritual becomes performance? What is preserved when tradition adapts to modernity? These questions are embedded in the brushstrokes, even when not explicitly stated.
