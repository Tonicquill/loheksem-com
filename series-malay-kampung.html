Painting Series

Malay Kampung

Traditional wooden houses on stilts, community rituals, and the textures of rural Malaysian life—preserved through acrylic painting before modern development erased them from the landscape.

The Malay Kampung Series (1994–2000) represents Loh Ek Sem's first major body of work after leaving his advertising career to pursue fine art. Driven by an urgent need to document the kampung (village) life he remembered from his youth, Sem produced large-scale acrylic paintings that function as both art and cultural preservation.

Each painting depicts scenes that were commonplace in 1970s–1980s Malaysia but have since disappeared: attap-roofed wooden houses, children bathing at the well, fishing villages along the coast, communal cooking during festivals. These are not nostalgic fantasies—they are documentary records painted from memory and reference photographs.

The series includes 12 major works, ranging from intimate scenes (50 × 70cm) to monumental canvases (137 × 214cm). All paintings are executed in acrylic on canvas, chosen for its durability and ability to capture both fine detail and broad gestural strokes.

Cultural Context

Understanding Kampung Architecture

Traditional Malay kampung houses were designed for the tropical climate and available materials. Key features include:

  • Attap roof — Thatched roofing made from dried palm leaves (nipah or rumbia). Naturally insulating and waterproof, requiring replacement every 5–10 years.
  • Rumah tiang — House on stilts. The elevated floor protected against flooding, allowed airflow underneath, and provided space for livestock (chickens, ducks).
  • Anjung — Covered veranda at the front of the house. Used for receiving guests, drying crops, and evening socializing with neighbors.
  • Seresan — Rear extension of the house, typically used for cooking and domestic work. Connected to the main house by a covered walkway.

Kampung Community Life

The kampung was more than a collection of houses—it was a social unit defined by mutual assistance and shared rituals:

  • Gotong-royong — Communal work. Villagers gathered to build houses, harvest crops, or prepare for festivals. Labor was exchanged without payment; reciprocity was assumed.
  • Kenduri — Communal feast held for weddings, circumcisions, or religious celebrations. The entire village was fed; hosting a successful kenduri was a mark of social standing.
  • Perigi (well) — The communal water source. Before piped water, the well was where women gathered daily to collect water for cooking, bathing, and washing. It functioned as an informal communication hub.

Why This Series Matters

By the 1990s, rapid development was replacing kampung villages with modern housing estates. The wooden houses were demolished, the attap roofs replaced with zinc, the communal spaces privatized. Sem's paintings preserve these scenes not as nostalgia, but as documentation—a visual archive for future generations who will never experience kampung life firsthand.

The series also challenges the assumption that "modernization" is always progress. What is lost when community spaces are privatized? When self-built houses are replaced with cookie-cutter developments? When gotong-royong is replaced with paid labor? These questions are embedded in the paintings, even when not explicitly stated.
