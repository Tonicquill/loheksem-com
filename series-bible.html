Scripture & Faith

Bible Series

Biblical narratives and scripture scenes rendered with documentary detail — exploring faith, spirituality, and the timeless stories that have shaped moral consciousness across centuries.

The Bible Series represents Loh Ek Sem's engagement with scripture and religious narrative. These paintings depict key biblical scenes — from the Nativity and the Good Shepherd to the Crucifixion and Resurrection — rendered with the same attention to cultural and historical detail that characterizes his work across all series.

Working primarily in oil on canvas, Sem brings a Southeast Asian perspective to these universal stories. The familiar parables and narratives are depicted with an authenticity that transcends cultural boundaries, making the sacred accessible to viewers of all backgrounds. The series spans both New Testament narratives and symbolic religious imagery, each piece inviting contemplation of faith, sacrifice, and redemption.

The Good Shepherd

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 91cm x 116cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." (John 10:11)

Theological Meaning: In Reformed theology, the Good Shepherd represents Christ's particular love for His elect sheep. The Father gave these specific sheep to the Son before the foundation of the world, and Christ knows each by name. His shepherding is effectual—He loses none of those given to Him (John 6:39). The shepherd's rod and staff represent both protection and guidance, as Christ not only dies for His sheep but actively sanctifies and keeps them by His grace.

Practical Takeaway: For believers today, this doctrine provides profound comfort. Your salvation does not ultimately depend on your ability to hold onto God, but on Christ's faithful shepherding. When you wander, He seeks you. When you're weak, He carries you. The sheep hear His voice and follow—not because they're naturally obedient, but because the Shepherd makes them willing in the day of His power.

The Prodigal Son

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 81cm x 101cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son and threw his arms around him and kissed him." (Luke 15:20)

Theological Meaning: From a Reformed perspective, this parable illuminates the doctrine of effectual calling and regeneration. The son's return wasn't merely his own idea—the father's love initiated the restoration. Similarly, in salvation, God's grace precedes our response. The father running to meet his son while "still a long way off" pictures prevenient grace. The robe, ring, and sandals represent imputed righteousness—we're not just forgiven, but clothed in Christ's righteousness and adopted as sons.

Practical Takeaway: This parable confronts two errors: thinking we're too sinful for God's grace (the younger brother's shame) and thinking we're too good for it (the older brother's resentment). The gospel says both brothers need grace. Whether you've lived in reckless sin or moral obedience, you come to the Father the same way—by His grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone.

The Last Supper

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 76cm x 101cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." (Matthew 26:28)

Theological Meaning: In Reformed theology, the Last Supper establishes the New Covenant—parallel to but superior to the Old Covenant at Sinai. At Sinai, Moses sprinkled blood saying "This is the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:8); here Christ fulfills that type. The bread and cup represent His body broken and blood shed—the substance to which Passover pointed. Reformed theology emphasizes that Christ's words "This is my body" mean "This represents my body"—the sign points to the reality of His substitutionary atonement, not a physical transformation.

Practical Takeaway: The Lord's Supper is a means of grace where believers feed on Christ by faith. It's not merely a memorial but a spiritual banquet where the risen Christ meets with His people. When you partake, you're proclaiming His death, receiving spiritual nourishment, and renewing your covenant commitment. The table is for sinners who know they need grace—not for the righteous who think they deserve it.

The Crucifixion

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 61cm x 91cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "But he was pierced for our transgressions, he was crushed for our iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was on him, and by his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5)

Theological Meaning: The crucifixion is the heart of Reformed soteriology. Christ's death was substitutionary—He took the place of sinners, bearing the wrath of God that we deserved. It was also particular—He died to actually save His people, not merely make salvation possible. The cry "It is finished" (John 19:30) means the atonement is complete; nothing can be added. In Reformed theology, the cross isn't Plan B but God's eternal purpose—Christ was "slain from the creation of the world" (Revelation 13:8).

Practical Takeaway: The doctrine of substitutionary atonement transforms how you view both sin and grace. Your sins were real debts that required real payment—Christ's blood. But that payment was sufficient and final. When Satan accuses you, when guilt haunts you, when you feel unworthy—the answer isn't to minimize your sin but to magnify Christ's sacrifice. The cross says your sin is worse than you imagined, but God's grace in Christ is deeper still.

The Nativity

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 76cm x 101cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "And without controversy great is the mystery of godliness: He was manifested in the flesh, vindicated in the Spirit, seen by angels, preached among the nations, believed on in the world, taken up in glory." (1 Timothy 3:16)

Theological Meaning: The Incarnation is central to Reformed theology—the eternal Son took on human nature while remaining fully divine. This hypostatic union (two natures in one person) was necessary for salvation: as man, Christ could represent humanity and die; as God, His sacrifice had infinite worth. The manger reveals God's humility—He who fills heaven and earth entered it as a babe. Reformed theology emphasizes that Christ's humility began at conception and continued through His entire life of obedience, not just His death.

Practical Takeaway: The Incarnation means God understands human weakness—not by observation, but by experience. He was tempted, suffered, and knows your frame. But more than comfort, the Incarnation assures us of salvation's certainty. Your mediator isn't a distant deity but the God-man who bore your sins and now intercedes for you at the Father's right hand. The baby in the manger grew to be the Savior on the cross—and the King who reigns forever.

The Resurrection

Medium: Watercolor on archival paper

Dimensions: 81cm x 116cm

Status: Retained by Artist

Key Verse: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ! According to his great mercy, he has caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead." (1 Peter 1:3)

Theological Meaning: In Reformed theology, the Resurrection is God's vindication of Christ's atoning work. Romans 4:25 says He "was raised for our justification"—the Father's declaration that the debt was paid in full. Christ's resurrection is also the firstfruits (1 Corinthians 15:20)—guaranteeing the resurrection of all who are in Him. Union with Christ means believers are already spiritually resurrected (Ephesians 2:5-6), seated with Him in heavenly places. The resurrection demonstrates Christ's victory over sin, death, and Satan—the kingdoms of darkness are doomed to fall.

Practical Takeaway: The resurrection means your faith isn't in a dead martyr but a living Savior who intercedes for you now. When you sin, you have an advocate. When you suffer, you have a high priest who sympathizes. When you doubt, remember: the tomb is empty. The same power that raised Christ from the dead is at work in you (Ephesians 1:19-20). Your labor in the Lord isn't in vain because death isn't the end—it's the doorway to glory.
